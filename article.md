# From Scrolling to Shopping: How Social Media Is Changing Consumer Buying Behavior

**By Likhita Sambhana**
*Marketing & Consumer Behavior | BBA Student*

> **We no longer always need a reason to buy something. Sometimes, we simply need to see it.**

Most of us don't open social media intending to shop.

We open it to watch a few reels, discover something interesting, see what others are doing, or simply pass a few minutes. Yet somewhere between one scroll and the next, we may encounter a product we suddenly want a skincare product, a piece of jewellery, a restaurant, or a gadget we had never considered before.

I have experienced this myself. I once bought a chain simply because I saw it on Instagram and thought it looked pretty. I also bought a lip balm after seeing an influencer use it in a video. I wasn't searching for either product when I opened the app.

That made me wonder:

**If I wasn't looking for these products, where did the desire to buy them come from?**

This question reflects a broader shift in consumer behavior. Social media has not eliminated traditional needs, but it has created another route to consumption one where **discovery can come before demand**.

---

## From Need to Discovery

The traditional consumer journey can be understood as:

**Need → Search → Evaluate → Purchase**

A consumer recognizes a need, searches for possible solutions, compares alternatives, and eventually makes a purchase.

Social media has made this journey less linear.

Today, the process can also look like:

**Exposure → Curiosity → Desire → Validation → Purchase**

A person may see a skincare routine without searching for skincare. A food video may create interest in a restaurant they had never considered. A fashion reel may introduce a style that was never on their shopping list.

The product appears first. The desire follows.

From a marketing perspective, this is significant. Brands are no longer communicating only with consumers who are actively searching for their products. They can also reach people who may not yet know that a product is relevant to them.

**The marketplace has moved closer to the moment of attention.**

---

## The Psychology Behind the Scroll

Why can a short video make an unfamiliar product suddenly feel desirable?

Consumer psychology provides part of the answer.

**Social proof** can make a product feel more credible when we see positive reviews, comments, recommendations, and other people using it.

**Relatability** can make recommendations feel more personal when they come from creators whose lifestyles or experiences seem familiar to us.

**Aspirational marketing** connects products with lifestyles or identities that consumers may want to associate with themselves.

And repeated exposure can make unfamiliar products feel increasingly familiar.

This is why social media rarely presents a product as just a product.

A chain can become part of an aesthetic.

A lip balm can become part of a creator's daily routine.

A laptop can represent productivity.

A restaurant can represent an experience worth sharing.

**The product is not being communicated alone. The meaning surrounding the product is communicated with it.**

For marketers, understanding this difference is important. Consumers are not only evaluating what a product does. They are also considering how the product fits into their lives.

---

## Why Content Can Feel More Persuasive Than an Advertisement

My experience with the lip balm also made me think about influencer marketing.

If a brand had simply shown me an advertisement saying, *“Buy this lip balm,”* I might have ignored it.

But seeing someone actually use the product felt different.

That difference is important in modern digital marketing.

Research on influencer marketing has found that factors such as credibility, content characteristics, authenticity, and the relationship audiences develop with creators can affect consumer responses. A large meta-analysis published in the *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* also found that influencer marketing can significantly affect consumer engagement and purchase-related outcomes, although its effectiveness varies depending on the influencer, content, audience, product, and platform.

For me, the appeal was not simply the recommendation. The video gave me information while also entertaining me.

This is why I believe **content-led marketing can be more engaging than direct selling**.

People may not always want to be told to buy something. But they are often willing to learn, watch, compare, and discover.

The challenge for marketers is therefore not simply to promote a product, but to make the content surrounding that product valuable enough to deserve attention.

---

## The Algorithm as a Digital Matchmaker

There is another part of social media marketing that I find fascinating and sometimes a little creepy.

Sometimes I think about something, and after watching only a few reels, I suddenly see content or an advertisement related to it.

It can feel as though the platform somehow knows what I was thinking.

Of course, this does not mean that social media can literally read our thoughts. Recommendation systems use signals from our activity and interactions to personalize the content we see.

But the experience still reveals something important about modern digital marketing:

**relevance can now be delivered to consumers instead of being actively searched for. **

The algorithm learns what captures our attention, while marketers try to create content that fits into those patterns of interest.

This changes the marketer's question from:

> **“How do we advertise our product?” **

to:

> **“How do we become relevant enough to earn a place in the consumer's attention?”**

That is not simply a technological change. It is a change in how marketing and consumer discovery interact.

---

## Are We Choosing, or Are We Being Nudged?

This is where the conversation becomes more interesting.

I don't think social media completely controls what people buy. Consumers still compare prices, read reviews, consider alternatives, and make their own decisions.

But social media can influence **what enters our consideration in the first place**.

A product may appear repeatedly in our feed. A creator we trust may use it. Other consumers may recommend it. The platform may make purchasing convenient.

Eventually, something that was completely irrelevant to us can become something we are seriously considering.

That does not necessarily mean consumers are being manipulated.

It means the **environment surrounding the decision has changed**.

And that distinction matters for marketers.

The goal of digital marketing should not simply be to push consumers toward a transaction. It should be to understand what creates attention, interest, trust, and value while maintaining transparency with the audience.

Research on social commerce in India has found that trust in social media influencers and online communities can play an important role in purchase intentions.

So the relationship between consumer and marketer is becoming less about simply **selling** and more about **earning attention and trust**.

---

## What This Means for Modern Marketers

For marketers, the shift from search to discovery creates both an opportunity and a responsibility.

Consumers are surrounded by content. A direct advertisement is competing with entertainment, education, personal stories, reviews, and conversations.

This makes several principles increasingly important:

### **1. Earn attention, don't demand it.**

A consumer's attention has to be earned through relevance, creativity, or value.

### **2. Educate before you persuade.**

Useful information can create interest without immediately demanding a purchase.

### **3. Build trust before pushing conversion.**

Authenticity, transparency, reviews, and genuine experiences can influence how consumers evaluate a brand.

### **4. Make products meaningful, not merely visible.**

Consumers often connect with what a product represents as much as what it does.

### **5. Respect the consumer's intelligence.**

Good marketing does not require hiding the fact that persuasion is taking place. Sustainable digital marketing should create value while being honest about commercial intent.

For me, this is where modern marketing becomes especially interesting.

**The goal is no longer simply to make people see a product. It is to make the product worth noticing.**

---

## Conclusion: When Did We Start Wanting It?

Social media has certainly made shopping more convenient. But its bigger influence may be something more subtle.

It has changed the way products enter our minds.

We can discover products before we need them.

We can develop interest before we search.

We can learn from creators we have never met.

We can use reviews and social proof to reduce uncertainty.

And sometimes, we can buy something simply because a short video made it look appealing.

This does not make social media marketing inherently good or bad.

It makes it **powerful**.

And with that power comes a responsibility for marketers to understand not only how to influence consumers, but also how to create genuine value for them.

As consumers, perhaps the question is no longer simply:

> *“What do I need to buy?” *

It can also be:

> **“What have I started wanting and why?” **

Perhaps that is the most interesting change social media has brought to consumer behavior: **we are not only searching for products anymore; sometimes, products are finding their way into what we want.**
